On Numbers and Machines

Ada Lovelace
15 July 2026

Abstract

The Analytical Engine does not occupy common ground with mere calculating machines. It holds a position wholly its own, for it can act upon symbols as readily as upon numbers. Consider a simple relation such as $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$, which binds together the most fundamental constants of analysis in a single expression. This identity is often attributed to Euler and is celebrated for uniting $e$, $i$, $\pi$, $1$, and $0$ in one line. Such a relation is not merely computed; it is manipulated.

When a machine is furnished with a rule for generating each term of a series, it may evaluate expressions of considerable depth. A convenient example is the sum of an infinite geometric progression, which converges whenever the common ratio is smaller than unity:

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} ar^{n} = \frac{a}{1 - r}, \qquad |r| < 1.$$

From relations of this kind we may state and prove results with full rigour. The following classical fact requires nothing beyond elementary reasoning.

The real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ are uncountable.
Suppose, for contradiction, that $\mathbb{R}$ were countable. Then the interval $[0, 1]$ would be countable as well, and we could enumerate its elements as $x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots$. Construct a number $y \in [0,1]$ whose $n$-th digit differs from the $n$-th digit of $x_n$. Then $y$ differs from every $x_n$, contradicting the assumption that the list was complete. Hence $\mathbb{R}$ cannot be countable.

The engine, then, is not bounded to arithmetic. It is a general instrument for the manipulation of quantity and symbol alike — a distinction whose consequences we are only beginning to appreciate.1
